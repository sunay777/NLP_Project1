\section{Abstract}
{
\bf
\looseness=-1
We study model collapse inside a mechanism we can read: the induction circuit of \citet{singh2024needs}. A two-layer attention-only transformer learns an in-context symbol task and is retrained for up to 20 generations on pools that mix a fraction $\lambda$ of real data with data sampled from its predecessor. In the main sweep the circuit survives: test accuracy never falls below 0.979 and ablating layer 1 costs 0.66--0.76 accuracy in every generation. What degrades is the model's free generative choice. The probability it places on symbols absent from the context follows a two-parameter inheritance law, $m_{g+1} = a(1-\lambda)m_g + \varepsilon$ ($a = 0.951$, $R^2 = 0.994$), which predicts held-out real-data fractions. Removing those off-context samples from the synthetic pools cuts the gain $a$ to 0.19, and training-data attribution finds them 12--23$\times$ over-represented among the most influential examples: the errors are carried by specific synthetic samples, not by a damaged circuit. A pre-registered extension of the law to higher sampling temperatures failed four of five tests. Collapse ran away only without real data, and the circuit then broke once correct labels in the training pool fell to chance level.
}

\section{Introduction} \label{Intro}
\looseness=-1
Language models adapt to patterns shown only in their prompt, and much of this in-context learning (ICL) is attributed to \emph{induction heads}: a previous-token head copies each token's predecessor into its position, and an induction head one layer later attends from the current token to an earlier match and copies what followed it \citep{olsson2022context}. \citet{singh2024needs} isolate this circuit in a minimal symbol-copying task. Separately, models trained recursively on generated data suffer \emph{model collapse}, losing the tails of the data distribution \citep{shumailov2023curse,shumailov2024ai}; collapse is avoided when enough real data remains \citep{bertrand2023stability} or when data is accumulated rather than replaced \citep{gerstgrasser2024model}. These lines of work have not met. We ask whether recursive training damages the ICL circuit itself or only what the circuit produces, and we make a quantitative, pre-registered prediction about when collapse runs away.

\subsubsection{Background}
\looseness=-1
Attention patterns suggest a circuit but are not causal evidence, so we follow \citet{conmy2023towards} and ablate heads. To trace collapse back to specific training examples we use gradient-based data attribution \citep{pruthi2020estimating}, in the spirit of \citet{chen2026mechanistic}.

\section{Methods}
\looseness=-1
\textbf{Task and model.} Each sequence gives $K{=}4$ symbols (from 16) a random label (from 6). A study block lists the pairs and a query block repeats the symbols in an independent order; the model predicts each label, which only content-based matching can solve. Unlike \citet{singh2024needs} (two pairs, image symbols), we use tokens and query every symbol in shuffled order, so no positional shortcut works. The \emph{base} model predicts only the query labels. The \emph{extended} model then freely generates one more in-context symbol ($sq2$, uniform over the four in real data) and its label; $sq2$ is the degree of freedom that can collapse. The model has 2 attention-only layers of 4 heads ($d{=}64$, learned and rotary positions, tied embeddings, cosine learning-rate decay; 35k parameters). Hyper-parameters were chosen on validation loss and confirmed once on a held-out test seed.

\textbf{Collapse pipeline.} Generation 0 trains on a 20k-sequence real pool. Generation $g$ trains a \emph{fresh} model on a 20k pool with a fraction $\lambda$ of real sequences and the rest sampled from generation $g{-}1$ at temperature $T{=}1$. Prompts stay real and all answers are model-generated (\emph{prompt-real} regime). Each pool is split 90/10 into train and validation; test data is fresh real data. A generation must pass a strict gate (validation accuracy and a causal layer-1 check) or is retrained, which happened 20 times in 680 main-sweep generations with no final failures. We sweep $\lambda \in \{0, 0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 1\}$ for 12 generations, with 10 seeds for the extended model (20 generations at $\lambda{=}0$) and 5 for the base model.

\textbf{Metrics.} \emph{Off-context mass}, the probability $p(sq2 \mid \text{context})$ places on symbols not in the context, measures hallucination; query NLL on real data measures confidence. The circuit is measured causally, as the accuracy lost when every layer-1 head is mean-ablated (at most about 0.83, down to chance at 1/6). This is agnostic to how the circuit is implemented, which matters because 5\% of generations use a non-canonical variant that attention probes misread. We fit the inheritance law on $\lambda \in \{0, 0.25, 1\}$ and test it on $\{0.1, 0.5\}$. Interventions remove off-context or wrong-label samples from the synthetic pools, accumulate pools across generations, change the pool size, inject a one-off 5\% label-noise pulse, and vary depth, width and task size.

\textbf{Pre-registration.} From one calibration seed, the law was fitted per temperature $T \in \{0.7, 1.3, 1.6\}$. It predicts a critical fraction $\lambda^* = 1 - 1/a(T)$ whenever $a(T) > 1$. Five pass/fail tests were committed to git before the confirmatory runs (5 seeds per cell).

\section{Results}
\looseness=-1
\textbf{The circuit survives; its outputs do not.} At generation 0 (test accuracy 0.998) a layer-0 head sends 0.79 of each label's attention to the preceding symbol and a layer-1 head 0.93 of each query's attention to its match. Accuracy then stays $\geq$0.979 in every generation, and the accuracy lost by ablating layer 1 is 0.71 at generation 0 and 0.69--0.74 at the last generation for every $\lambda$ (Figure~\ref{fig:headline}c). Confidence erodes instead: without real data, query NLL rises from 0.006 to 0.030 by generation 19 while accuracy is 0.996 (Figure~\ref{fig:headline}b). The base model, with no free choice, shows only this effect: at $\lambda{=}0$ its accuracy stays 1.000 while query NLL rises from 0.0002 to 0.0058 by generation 11.

\textbf{An inheritance law.} Off-context mass grows from 0.013 to 0.138 by generation 19 without real data, and to 0.057, 0.035, 0.021 and 0.012 by generation 11 at $\lambda = 0.1, 0.25, 0.5$ and 1. The law fits all 630 transitions with $a = 0.951$ (95\% CI 0.942--0.959), $\varepsilon = 0.0105$ and $R^2 = 0.994$. Fitted without $\lambda \in \{0.1, 0.5\}$, it predicts them with RMSE 0.003, within one seed SD at 68\% of generations (Figure~\ref{fig:headline}a). It overshoots the plateau: for $\lambda{=}0$ it predicts $m^* = 0.21$, while mass is 0.13 at generations 16--19 and still rising. The gain holds across 3- and 4-layer, MLP and wider models ($a = 0.94$--$1.04$, $R^2 \geq 0.98$) and is lower on larger tasks (0.84 at $K{=}6$, $R^2 = 0.80$; 0.85 with 32 symbols); a one-layer model cannot learn the task (accuracy 0.64).

\textbf{Specific synthetic samples carry the errors.} Removing off-context samples from each synthetic pool cuts the gain from 0.95 to 0.19 (95\% CI $-0.02$--0.43), while removing wrong labels leaves it at 0.95 (0.93--0.97). Attribution agrees: off-context samples are 12--23$\times$ over-represented among the top 1\% most influential examples. Accumulating pools lowers the gain to 0.62 (0.52--0.71), and a 5k pool raises it to 1.03 (1.02--1.04, vs 0.91 for matched 20k runs), so mass runs away to 0.58 without real data. A label-noise pulse decays at the predicted rate with real data (half-life 2.2 vs 2.1 generations at $\lambda{=}0.25$; 0.97 vs 0.93 at 0.5) but far more slowly without it (33 vs 14).

\textbf{The pre-registered prediction failed.} Only the qualitative trade-off held (Table~\ref{tab:prereg}, test 5); the predicted critical fractions ($\lambda^* \approx 0.30$ at $T{=}1.3$, 0.52 at 1.6) do not exist: mass exceeded 0.5 in every seed at $\lambda{=}0$, yet no seed ran away in the six cells with 20--50\% real data predicted to.

\textbf{When collapse runs away, the circuit breaks.} In those runs the share of correct pool labels fell to 0.15 ($T{=}1.3$) and 0.05 ($T{=}1.6$) by generation 9, but the circuit outlasted them. At $T{=}1.6$ it was intact at generation 4 with 26\% correct labels (accuracy 0.93, layer-1 effect 0.67) and gone at generation 5 with 15\% (0.37 and 0.06); at $T{=}1.3$ it broke at the same share (0.46 and 0.20). Both breaks came as correct labels fell to chance (1/6).

\section{Discussion}
\looseness=-1
In the prompt-real regime, bounded collapse leaves the induction circuit intact: every self-generated query is still answered by copying from a real context, which re-teaches the same computation. Collapse concentrates in the free choice, as off-context hallucination inherited through off-context samples. The circuit is lost only when copying stops paying, once correct answers are no more common than chance. The failed pre-registration bounds the linear account: at high temperature real data damps errors far more than a constant gain allows, so the law describes collapse locally but does not locate tipping points (its single-seed calibration also underestimated the gain at $T{=}1$: 0.86 vs 0.95). Survival also depends on supervising only the answers: with every token supervised and no real data, the layer-1 effect falls from 0.83 to 0.71 and accuracy to 0.88 by generation 9, even with real prompts. Other limits: the 35k-parameter model, 16 symbols, 5--10 seeds and at most 20 generations. Two lessons carry over: accuracy can hide eroding confidence, and a modest share of real data, or accumulating data, sharply limits what a model inherits.
